Fixed vs Adjustable Rate Mortgage

One gives you certainty, the other a lower start. Which matches your plans?

Related: What Is a Good Rate · Mortgage Payment
The short version
Fixed if you'll stay long; ARM if you'll move or refinance soon.

A fixed rate keeps your payment stable for the whole term. An ARM starts lower but can rise later. Choose based on your timeline and tolerance for change.

How an ARM works

An ARM like a 5/1 ARM keeps a fixed rate for the first 5 years, then adjusts once a year after that. The "5" is the fixed period; the "1" is the adjustment frequency. Common variants are 5/1, 7/1, and 10/1.

Rate caps protect you. ARMs have limits on how much the rate can change at each adjustment and over the life of the loan — so your payment can't spike without bound. Always ask your lender for the caps.

How to decide

  • Plan to stay 5+ years? A fixed rate removes the risk of future increases.
  • Moving or refinancing within 5–7 years? An ARM's lower initial rate can save real money.
  • Expect rates to fall? An ARM lets you benefit when it adjusts lower.
  • Prefer certainty? Fixed is the calm choice — the payment never surprises you.

Common questions

What is the difference between a fixed and adjustable rate mortgage?▾
A fixed-rate mortgage keeps the same rate for the entire term, so your payment stays the same. An ARM has a fixed rate for an initial period (like 5, 7, or 10 years), then adjusts periodically based on the market.
Which is better: fixed or adjustable?▾
It depends on how long you'll stay. If you'll keep the home for a short time, an ARM's lower initial rate can save money. If you'll stay for many years, a fixed rate protects you from future increases.
When should I choose an ARM?▾
An ARM can make sense if you plan to move or refinance before the fixed period ends, or if you expect rates to fall. Make sure you understand the caps on how much the rate can change.
What is a 5/1 ARM?▾
A 5/1 ARM has a fixed rate for the first 5 years, then adjusts once a year after that. The '5' is the fixed period; the '1' is how often it adjusts. Common variants include 7/1 and 10/1.
